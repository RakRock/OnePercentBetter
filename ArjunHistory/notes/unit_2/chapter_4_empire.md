# 👑 Chapter 4: The Expanding Empire and the Big Disagreement

Muhammad died in **June 632 CE**. He had spent more than 20 years spreading the word of Allah, and Arab nomads had answered that message because Islam brought order, justice, and hope of heaven. Then the community had no leader.

According to the traditions of the largest group of Muslims today, Muhammad had not named a successor and had not said how to choose one. Panic spread. **Abu Bakr**, Muhammad's father-in-law and trusted friend, spoke to calm people. His daughter **A'ishah** was Muhammad's wife. Abu Bakr's point was simple: Muhammad was a man and he was dead, but the God they worshiped lives forever. Leaders of that group chose Abu Bakr as the next leader.

## Abu Bakr, the first caliph

In 632, Abu Bakr became the first **caliph**, a title that means "successor." He promised to follow Muhammad's example closely.

The new community was shaky.

- Some clans on the Arabian Peninsula left Islam.
- Others refused to pay taxes.
- A few people even claimed to be new prophets.

Abu Bakr ruled for about two years. He used the army to pull the community back together, brought central Arabia under Muslim control, and began conquests of lands to the north that are now **Iraq** and **Syria**.

## The rightly guided caliphs

The next three caliphs were **Umar**, **Uthman**, and **Ali**. Like Abu Bakr, they were chosen from people who had known Muhammad and supported his mission. These four are the **Rightly Guided Caliphs**. That name comes from the belief that they used the **Quran** and Muhammad's own actions as their guide. Their government was called a **caliphate**.

## How big the empire got

The caliphs after Abu Bakr kept expanding and finished the conquest of **Persia**. By **661 CE**, the Muslim Empire was nearly four times larger than it had been when Muhammad died. It covered Southwest Asia and reached into North Africa. Growth came by conquest and by treaty.

The story did not stop in 661. Within about 100 years, the empire stretched from India, across North Africa, and into Spain (called **Al-Andalus**).

- The **Umayyad** dynasty took power in **661 CE** and moved the capital from **Medina** to **Damascus**, in newly conquered Syria.
- The **Abbasid** dynasty overthrew the Umayyads in **750 CE** and built a new capital, **Baghdad**, on the Tigris River.
- **Arabic** became the language of government, trade, and religion.
- The **Battle of Tours** in **732 CE** stopped Muslim armies from pushing farther into Western Europe.
- In Spain, **Cordoba** became a famous center of learning. **Berber** armies from North Africa were a major part of the force that entered Spain.

## Why the conquest worked

- Muslims saw victories as signs of Allah's support. Armies were disciplined, and their leaders were highly skilled. Skilled troops, including soldiers on camels, conquered a huge amount of land.
- Two tired empires north of Arabia, the **Byzantine** and **Persian** empires, had been fighting each other for a long time. Their armies were exhausted.
- Those empires had persecuted people who did not follow the rulers' religions. Many of those people welcomed Muslim armies as liberators.
- The Quran did not allow Muslims to force anyone to convert. Conquered people could keep their own religions.

## Life under Muslim rule

Cultures blended. Over time, many people chose Islam. They were drawn by its message of equality and by hope of heaven. There was a practical reason too: Muslims did not have to pay certain taxes.

Jews and Christians were **People of the Book**, also called **dhimmi**, or "protected people." They paid a yearly poll tax, the **jizya**, and in return they did not have to serve in the army. They could hold important jobs as officials and scholars. They were not allowed to try to convert other people.

Law based on the Quran and the Sunnah is called **sharia**.

## The team split: Sunni and Shi'a

Winning battles was easier than agreeing on one ruler.

In **656 CE**, rebels who opposed **Uthman** murdered him. That murder started a civil war. **Ali**, Muhammad's cousin and son-in-law, was the logical next caliph, but people challenged him too. In **661 CE**, Ali was assassinated. Choosing a caliph by agreement died with him.

A family called the **Umayyads** took power and made rule **hereditary**: the next ruler would come from the same family and inherit the job. They moved the capital from Medina to Damascus. Some Arab Muslims thought Damascus was too far away. Later critics said the Umayyads left the simpler Bedouin ways of the early caliphs, lived in luxury, and still claimed religious authority. Those choices split the community and reopened the question of who should lead.

Most Muslims accepted the Umayyads because they wanted peace. A minority refused. They believed the caliph had to be a relative of the Prophet.

- **Shi'a** means the "party" of Ali. Its members are called **Shiites**. They say only Ali, among the early caliphs, was the legitimate ruler. Later rulers, called **imams**, should be descendants of Muhammad and Ali. Their sources are the Quran, the Sunnah, and the teachings of the imams.
- **Sunni** means followers of the **Sunnah**, Muhammad's example. Sunnis accepted the elected caliphs and say all four of the first caliphs were rightful rulers. Any Muslim who follows Muhammad's example may lead. Their sources are the Quran and the Sunnah. Sunnis are the large majority of Muslims today. They see caliphs as human leaders who can make mistakes.

The split became permanent. Opposition to the Umayyads later helped bring their caliphate down. The argument was about leadership, not about whether there is one God. Both branches read the same Quran and share the Five Pillars. That division still causes conflict in the Muslim world today.

In **680 CE**, the **Battle of Karbala** deepened the split. Muhammad's grandson **Hussein** was killed there. Shi'a Muslims remember him each year on **Ashura**.

**Sufism** is a mystical path in Islam that focuses on a personal, direct experience of God through prayer, poetry, and meditation, rather than on who holds political power.
